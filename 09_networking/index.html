Project Spike

Team: Chloe · Gurmani · Matthew

Spike, the pacifist pirate dragon, has sailed oceans and soared through deep blue skies. Somewhere along the way he lost an eye, shed his wings, and gained a little wisdom.

He no longer breathes fire — these days he blows cool air on everyone around him, doing his small part for peace in a hot summer. And yes, he's remote controlled.

Spike is a remote-controlled articulated dragon that blows cooling air from his mouth, driven wirelessly by a custom rotary encoder PCB over ESP-NOW. His eye blinks in sync with the remote, faster as you turn the encoder, off when you click it. Bearing his pirate patch, he promotes peace.

How It Works

Remote

Rotary Encoder PCB

A custom-milled PCB from Week 8: XIAO ESP32-C3, a clickable rotary encoder, and an LED. Click to pair, turn to change the blink rate.

Protocol

ESP-NOW

Peer-to-peer radio communication, no Wi-Fi router required. Messages flow REQUEST → ACK → CONTROL, carrying an ON/OFF state and an INTENSITY value (0–100) for the blink rate.

Dragon

XIAO ESP32-C3

Receives the control signal, drives the fan through a custom motor driver (2N3904 transistor + IRFP460 MOSFET), and blinks the LED eye accordingly.

Output

Air Pump + LED Eye

A 12V fan pushes air through the dragon's mouth, and a single LED in one eye blinks in sync with the remote. The other eye: a pirate patch.

Enclosure

The Rock

A vacuum-formed PETG dome over a red wooden ball, dressed with real soil, stones, and weeds collected outside the Science Center.

Identity

Project Spike

Team T-shirts featuring Chloe's logo, designed in Inkscape, vinyl cut, and heat pressed. "Spawned in Harvard" — because it was.

Part 1: The Dragon

Gurmani + Chloe · Blender · Maya · SolidWorks · Prusa MK4S

01

Chloe's Blender Model

Chloe modeled the dragon head in Blender — spiky and characterful enough to deserve a name of its own: Spike. That spiked crown is what gave the whole project its identity.

Chloe's dragon head — complex mesh I imported into SolidWorks

02

Iteration — Maya Retopo + SolidWorks Shell

Pivoted

I imported the Blender mesh into Maya to retopologize it for cleaner geometry, then brought it into SolidWorks to shell it into a hollow body for the electronics and airflow. Neither step got me there. SolidWorks couldn't resolve a clean offset at any distance, even with 21+ faces selected for the shell — the mesh was simply too complex. Maya's retopology tools hit the same wall: they couldn't simplify the spike geometry without destroying the silhouette that made the design distinct. Time to try a different approach.

SolidWorks offset attempt — all faces selected, still couldn't resolve the complex spike geometry

Computer hanged and software crashed multiple times

03

Plan B — Print-in-Place Articulated Dragon

This worked

Found a print-in-place articulated dragon on MakerWorld that prints fully jointed with no supports needed. The new plan: print the whole body in orange PLA, remove the stock head, and swap in Chloe's Blender head. I set up the G-code in PrusaSlicer, using the paint-on support feature to add supports only where the new head needed them, keeping the articulated body support-free.

Prusa MK4S mid-print — articulated body segments forming at 1% complete, 12h 44m remaining

04

Drilling + Electronics Integration

Removed the supports from the printed head, then drilled three holes: one running from the mouth to the underside (for the air tube), and one through each eye, both converging at the same exit point on the underside (for the LED wiring). The eye LED blinks in sync with the remote — faster as the rotary encoder turns, off when it's clicked again.

Part 2: The Rock

Gurmani · Vacuum forming · PETG · Natural materials

The pump needed somewhere to hide. The solution: build a rock — a convincing one, made from a vacuum-formed dome and real debris collected outside the Harvard Science Center.

01

Vacuum Forming the Dome

Found a red wooden pendulum ball in the lab and vacuum-formed a PETG dome over it, using the same process as the Week 8 PCB cover. Cut the dome free and scored an opening at the top for the dragon's body to sit in, with room for the wiring to exit.

Left: red wooden ball positive · Right: PETG dome after forming — ball and cover side by side

02

Collecting the "Rock" Materials

Ran outside the Harvard Science Center — literally — and collected soil, pebbles, stones, and weeds. Brought it all back in a bag and hot-glued it to the outside of the PETG dome to build up a mossy rock surface.

Left: collected materials from outside Harvard Science Centre · Right: finished rock enclosure

03

Assembly — Dragon on Rock

With the pump hidden inside the dome, the air tube routes through the rock surface, up through the dragon's body, and out through the drilled mouth hole. Small pieces of light plastic sit in front of the mouth and flutter when the air blows. The dragon's articulated body drapes naturally over the rock.

Preparation for final assembly: dragon body on rock enclosure

Part 3: The Brand

Chloe + Gurmani · Inkscape · Vinyl cutter · Heat press

If you're going to present a remote-controlled pirate dragon at Harvard, you should probably wear matching shirts.

01

Logo Design + Vinyl Cutting

Chloe designed the Project Spike logo in Inkscape: the dragon silhouette set against a red sun with yellow rays, under the tagline "PROJECT SPIKE — Spawned in Harvard." It was vinyl cut in multiple layers — yellow for the sun rays, red for the circle, navy for the dragon and text — with "Spawned in Harvard" cut separately as its own text strip.

Left: vinyl layers laid out for assembly · Right: "Spawned in Harvard" cut and weeded

02

Heat Press

Watched a few tutorials, assembled the layered vinyl on the shirts, and heat pressed each one on the lab's machine — three shirts, one press each. The 3D printed dragon silhouette from SolidWorks ended up embedded in the logo too, printed flat and pressed directly onto the fabric as a raised element on top of the vinyl.
