Project Spike

Team: Gurmani · Chloe · Matthew

Spike is a remote-controlled articulated dragon who blows cooling air from his mouth, controlled wirelessly via a custom rotary encoder PCB over ESP-NOW. His eye blinks in sync with the remote — faster when you turn the encoder, off when you click. He wears a pirate patch because we ran out of LEDs. He promotes peace.

Spike, the pacifist pirate dragon, has voyaged oceans and deep blue skies. He has lost an eye at sea and shed his wings while gaining wisdom along his way.

He no longer breathes fire — he blows air onto the beings around him to cool them down in this hot summer and promote peace. And he's remote controlled.

How It Works

Remote

Rotary Encoder PCB

Custom milled PCB from Week 8 — XIAO ESP32-C3, clickable encoder, LED. Click to pair, turn to increase blink rate.

Protocol

ESP-NOW

Peer-to-peer radio — no WiFi router needed. REQUEST → ACK → CONTROL messages. ON/OFF and INTENSITY (0–100) for blink rate.

Dragon

XIAO ESP32-C3

Receives control signal, drives fan via custom motor driver (2N3904 transistor + IRFP460 MOSFET), blinks LED eye.

Output

Air Pump + LED Eye

12V fan blows air through dragon mouth. Single LED in one eye blinks in sync with remote. Other eye: pirate patch.

Enclosure

The Rock

Vacuum formed PETG dome over a red wooden ball — filled with real soil, stones and weeds collected outside the Science Centre.

Part 1 — The Dragon

Gurmani + Chloe · Blender · Maya · SolidWorks · Prusa MK4S

01

Chloe's Blender Model

Chloe modelled the dragon head in Blender — spiky, characterful, with enough personality to deserve a name. Spike. The spiked crown is what gave the project its identity.

Chloe's dragon head — complex mesh imported into SolidWorks

02

Failed Attempts — Maya Retopo + SolidWorks Shell

Both failed

I imported the Blender mesh into Maya to retopologise it for cleaner geometry, then into SolidWorks to shell it — creating a hollow body for electronics and airflow. Both failed. The mesh was too complex for SolidWorks to offset the surfaces: 21+ faces selected, still couldn't compute a clean offset at any distance. Maya's retopo tools couldn't handle the spike geometry without destroying the silhouette.

SolidWorks offset surface failure — 21 faces selected, still couldn't resolve the complex spike geometry

03

Plan B — Print-in-Place Articulated Dragon

This worked

Found a print-in-place articulated dragon on MakerWorld that prints without supports and comes out fully jointed. The plan: print the full dragon in orange PLA, remove its existing head, replace with Chloe's Blender head. I set up the gcode in PrusaSlicer — added supports only to the Blender head using the paint-on support regions feature, leaving the articulated body support-free.

Prusa MK4S mid-print — articulated body segments forming at 1% complete, 12h 44m remaining

Fresh off the Prusa — full articulated dragon with Chloe's head, orange PLA

04

Drilling + Electronics Integration

Removed supports from the printed head. Drilled three holes: one from the mouth to the bottom surface (for the air valve tube), and one through each eye converging at the same bottom exit point (for the LED wiring). The LED in the eye blinks in sync with the remote's LED — faster as the rotary encoder turns, off when clicked again.

Part 2 — The Rock

Gurmani · Vacuum forming · PETG · Natural materials

The pump needed hiding. The solution: build a rock. A real enough looking rock, made from a vacuum formed dome and actual debris collected from outside the Harvard Science Centre.

01

Vacuum Forming the Dome

Found a red wooden pendulum ball in the lab. Vacuum formed a PETG dome over it — same process as the PCB cover from Week 8. Cut the dome out, scored the top to create an opening for the dragon body to sit in and wiring to exit.

Left: red wooden ball positive · Right: PETG dome after forming — ball and cover side by side

02

Collecting the "Rock" Materials

Ran outside the Harvard Science Centre — literally — and collected soil, pebbles, stones, and weeds from the ground. Brought them back in a bag and stuck them to the outside of the PETG dome using hot glue to simulate a mossy rock surface.

Left: collected materials from outside Harvard Science Centre · Right: finished rock enclosure

03

Assembly — Dragon on Rock

Pump hidden inside the dome. Air tube routed through the rock surface, up through the dragon body, through the drilled mouth hole. Small pieces of light plastic cut and placed in front of the mouth — they flutter when the air blows. The dragon's articulated body drapes naturally over the rock.

Spike assembled on his rock — pump concealed, tube routed through body to mouth

Part 3 — The Brand

Chloe + Gurmani · Inkscape · Vinyl cutter · Heat press

If you're going to present a remote-controlled pirate dragon at Harvard, you should probably wear matching shirts.

01

Logo Design + Vinyl Cutting

Chloe designed the Project Spike logo in Inkscape — the dragon silhouette against a red sun with yellow rays, "PROJECT SPIKE — Spawned in Harvard" tagline. Vinyl cut in multiple layers: yellow for the sun rays, red for the circle, navy for the dragon and text. "Spawned in Harvard" was cut separately as a text strip.

Left: vinyl layers laid out for assembly · Right: "Spawned in Harvard" cut and weeded

02

Heat Press

Watched tutorials, assembled the layered vinyl on the shirts, heat pressed using the lab's heat press machine. Three shirts, one press each. The 3D printed dragon silhouette from SolidWorks ended up embedded in the logo — printed flat and pressed directly onto the fabric as a raised element on top of the vinyl.
